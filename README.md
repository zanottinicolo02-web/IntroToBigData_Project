# IntroToBigData_Project
The goal of this project is for students to practice the concepts studied in the course by exploiting different datasets. 

Dataset Description (from main doc):
Our dataset combines two open sources on the U.S. power sector, both curated by the Public Utility Data Liberation Project (PUDL) and read directly from its public Parquet files. The first is the EPA’s Continuous Emissions Monitoring System (CEMS): an hourly record of gross load and CO₂, SO₂ and NOₓ emissions for every fossil-fuel unit above 25 MW, identified by plant and smokestack unit. We restrict it to the nine Northeast states and to eight sample years (2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2018, 2020, 2023, 2025), chosen to cover the transition of the power system while keeping the size manageable. (TO DISCUSS: I wanted to keep the number of tuples close to 50M while still having an interesting time span, so I chose some sample years instead of all of them and kept the 9 Northeast states. The alternatives are to reduce the states and use every year, or any other idea. For reference, all years for the 9 states would be 186M CEMS rows.)
The second source is EIA data, which describes the infrastructure behind those emissions: a plant table (one row per plant, with state, county and coordinates; we keep all Northeast plants, although only a subset appears in CEMS), a yearly generator table (technology, fuel and capacity, one row per generator and year) and a monthly table of generation and fuel consumption by plant, fuel and prime mover. The yearly generator and monthly generation tables are restricted to Northeast plants and to the same sample years.
The two sources use different identification systems. CEMS measures emissions at the level of the emissions unit (a smokestack, i.e. a boiler or turbine monitored by the EPA), while EIA describes generators, and the two do not map one-to-one: an EPA unit can feed several generators, and a generator can depend on several units. To connect them we include the EPA–EIA crosswalk, a bridge table that lists, for each EPA unit, the corresponding EIA boilers and generators. It is maintained by the EPA and rebuilt by PUDL for each year of EIA data, with unmatched rows removed. In the PUDL release we use, it is available only for 2018–2024, so among our sample years it covers 2018, 2020, and 2023. Where available, it allows us to attach generator attributes such as technology, fuel and capacity to hourly emissions, and therefore to study emissions by generator type rather than only by plant. Because the relationship is many-to-many, aggregations that cross it need care to avoid double counting. CEMS also carries the EIA plant ID directly, so plant-level analyses cover all sample years without the crosswalk. (TO DISCUSS: I included the crosswalk for possible queries by generator type. Since it covers only 3 of our sample years, we could leave it out and use plant_id_eia directly in all queries.)
Hourly measurements are matched to yearly or monthly attributes by truncating the timestamp, and all timestamps are in UTC. PUDL cleans and standardizes the data, but some IDs are missing in CEMS, and small or non-fossil units are not covered. In total, the dataset contains 51,154,632 hourly CEMS records, 4,133 plants, 33,585 yearly generator records, 224,648 monthly generation records and 3,340 crosswalk records, for 51,420,338 tuples.
